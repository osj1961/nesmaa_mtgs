Time: 1:00-1:15
Presenter: Josephine Reynes
Affiliation: Norwich University
Title: Why the Tutte Polynomial is Amazing
Abstract:
What do spanning trees, graph flows, graph coloring, and network reliability have in common? They can all be studied by a single polynomial! The Tutte polynomial is a remarkably powerful graph invariant that encodes a wide variety of combinatorial information. This talk will explore its construction and some of its evaluations through examples to examine seemingly unrelated information about a graph.

Time: 1:20-1:35
Presenter: Grace McCourt; Emily Heath, Alex Parker, Coy Schwieder, Shira Zerbib
Affiliation: University of Rhode Island
Title: Ramsey numbers in Kneser graphs
Abstract:
In this talk, we will give a brief history of Ramsey numbers, including recent breakthroughs. Then we will introduce the r-Kneser Ramsey number, which is the minimum n such that every red/blue edge-coloring of the Kneser graph KG(n,r) contains a red clique on s vertices or a blue clique on t vertices. We will give bounds on the r-Kneser Ramsey number as well as some small exact values. This is joint work with Emily Heath, Alex Parker, Coy Schwieder, and Shira Zerbib.

Time: 1:40-1:55
Presenter: Mark Mixer; Matthew Drum, Bryan Kaber, and Patrick Prudhomme
Affiliation: Wentworth Institute of Technology
Title: A Combinatorial Game on Paths and Caterpillars
Abstract:
The Game of Cycles is a two-player combinatorial game in which players take turns assigning directions to the edges of a graph, subject to restrictions that prevent the creation of sources and sinks. We introduce the mixed Game of Cycles, which allows some edges to be directed before play begins and permits disconnected graphs. This generalization provides a natural framework for studying game positions using Grundy values and decompositions into simpler games.

We develop structural tools that allow us to determine the Grundy value of any mixed path. In particular, we establish a sharp upper bound and characterize the unique family of mixed paths attaining it. We also introduce a pendant decomposition that yields a simple formula for the Grundy value of any caterpillar graph, depending only on the parity of the number of edges in its spine. 

These results illustrate how graph structure can reveal surprisingly simple winning strategies in combinatorial games.

Time: 1:00-1:15
Presenter: Michael Nathanson
Affiliation: Bentley University
Title: The ABCs of baseball's ABS
Abstract:
Major League Baseball has just concluded its innaugural season using the Automated Ball-Strike (ABS) Challenge System.
Similar to manager's replay challenges, teams are only allowed two unsuccessful challenges during a
game. Because ABS challenges are initiated by individual players, teams benefit from simple and clear criteria for when a challenges is advisable. We will use data from the 2026 season to consider the differential strategies of batters and catchers initiating challenges, building a simple theoretical model and making reasonable parameter estimates.

Time: 1:20-1:35
Presenter: Peter Staab; Rick Cleary, Babson College
Affiliation: Fitchburg State University
Title: It's raining 3s: Shooting streaks in the NBA
Abstract:
In recent decades, considerable statistical interest has focused on shooting streaks in basketball and the validity of the "hot hand" phenomenon—the belief that a player's probability of making a shot increases following a sequence of successful attempts. While most prior research evaluates individual performance, this talk investigates shooting streaks at the team level, focusing on three-point shooting data from the NBA, with a particular case study on the Boston Celtics. Using historical data analysis and probabilistic modeling, we evaluate whether entire teams exhibit "hot hand" behavior or if team-wide streaks align with random variation.

Time: 1:40-1:55
Presenter: Michael Arciero
Affiliation: University of Southern Maine
Title: Monte Carlo simulations for Power analysis; an application to Zero-inflated data
Abstract:
"Power analyis" is conducted when one wants to study the relationships between sample size, effect size, and power of some statistical test. In cases where formulas relating these quantities do exist, they require that one be able to estimate the distribution of some test statistic. We can perform the same analyses with much greater flexibility, and no data or distributional assumptions, through simulation. In this talk we demonstrate this approach for "zero inflated" hospital admissions data. Central to the approach is the use of a Cholesky decomposition trick to correlate individual patient variables. This talk does not assume any specialized statistics background.

Session 4 — Room RM 221
-----------------------

Time: 1:00-1:15
Presenter: Matthew Moore
Affiliation: Boston University
Title: A Differential Equations Model of What Really Harms Your Teeth
Abstract:
Ask people what harms their teeth and you will hear sugar, maybe salt. The answer is subtler: how often you eat, what you eat, and how long it lingers all matter, and two different kinds of damage are at work. We build a differential equations model in which each food is a short burst of constants (how readily bacteria ferment it, how long it clings, how acidic it is, how much saliva it stirs up), so a whole day of eating, foods alone, in sequence or together, becomes a piecewise-defined system. A fast clock follows the pH at the tooth surface over minutes; slow clocks follow the enamel over months. Averaging over the day reveals two very different long-term behaviours. Decay has a tipping point: below a critical habit the tooth recovers, beyond it no healthy equilibrium exists, and fluoride moves that threshold. Erosion from acidic drinks has no equilibrium at all and only accumulates. The model explains why sipping is worse than gulping, why diet soda still wears enamel away, and why salt itself is harmless, and it makes an accessible modeling project for a first course in differential equations.

Time: 1:20-1:35
Presenter: William Sanguinet
Affiliation: Worcester Polytechnic Institute
Title: Using web-based open calculators to create high-quality visualizations of mathematical objects in 2D and 3D
Abstract:
Over the past 20 years, several good free web-based calculators have been developed (Desmos3D, Geogebra3D, CalcPlot3D, etc.). Each calculator has various strengths and weaknesses and can be used to create stunning visuals of various mathematical concepts with varying levels of effort from the user. Recent additions of 3D capabilities, recursion, color functions, and matrix manipulation to Desmos have made it a particularly good choice for creating plots which demonstrate explicit algorithms for numerically approximating solutions to differential equations (Euler, RK45, etc.), optimization problems (Gradient descent, etc.), and other numerical methods. These graphs can be easily shared by embedding or copy-pasting a simple web address into an LMS. This presentation will focus on several recent additions to the Desmos calculator and demonstrate various recent classroom demos I have created using them. I will focus on sample examples that I have created for use in various classes such as multivariable vector calculus, differential equations, complex variables, and projective geometry.
