This research adds Whitton Pond to the Lakes Lay Monitoring Program (LLMP), which tracks the
water quality and ecosystem health of approximately 50 lakes across New Hampshire. Whitton
Pond, located in the towns of Albany and Madison, is a 164-acre freshwater lake with an average
depth of 12 feet and a maximum depth of 57 feet. We conducted weekly water quality testing,
collecting scientific data about the pond’s health. This included water transparency (water clarity
informs on algal populations, silt, and water color), temperature (thermal profile assesses the
stratification of the water column), chlorophyll (tracks microscopic phytoplankton populations),
dissolved water color (the natural background color can track climate-driven browning), phosphorus
(the limiting nutrient to growth of algae, an indicator of pollution and sediment erosion),
cyanobacteria (this algae harms aquatic ecosystems), and alkalinity (informs how water neutralizes
acidic pollution from acid rain and snowmelt).
This talk will share other analyses of the 2026 data from Whitton Pond. Analysis of the data collected
from the other bodies of water participating in the LLMP will place Whitton Pond into a broader
context. All told, this analysis provides insight into the natural capacity of the New Hampshire lake
system to respond to changing climate conditions.

Domenic Yacovone; Catherine Roberts
College of the Holy Cross

Take a standard Young tableau and its transpose. Glue the transpose to the lower left corner of the tableau so that the two pieces share no row and no column. Then slide the result into a straight shape using jeu de taquin. Usually the final shape depends on which tableau you started with. We show that rectangles behave differently. If the starting tableau is a rectangle with a rows and b columns, every choice of tableau produces the same final shape, and we describe that shape explicitly in terms of a and b. Our main result is that rectangles are the only shapes with this property: for every shape that is not a rectangle, there are two tableaux of that shape whose glued versions rectify to different shapes. Along the way, we show that the final shape is always the double of a shifted shape. This connects the problem to shifted tableaux, Greene’s theorem on increasing and decreasing subsequences, and Littlewood-Richardson coefficients.

John La Volpe; Hunter Markgraf, Colin Schwarz, Tyler Tavera, Ryan Kapoor
Chaminade High School

This research seeks to understand how social behavior spreads on social networks. This is important in many areas; understanding how smoking behavior spreads on social networks could have important implications for designing interventions to help reduce the prevalence of smoking. We consider two types of social behavior (e.g., smoking and non-smoking) and model how such behavior can spread in a population represented by a social network. Each vertex in the network represents an individual in the population, and edges between vertices represent social connections between the corresponding individuals. Each individual in the population (i.e., each vertex in the network) has one of the two states associated with it. We assume that behavior spreads through the network by individuals imitating the behavior of their social contacts (i.e., by individuals imitating the states of their neighbors in the network). This imitation process is implemented computationally as an agent-based model on the network.

Sei Wilkinson
University of Massachusetts Boston

A symmetric function is a formal power series of bounded degree in infinitely many variables that is invariant under permutations of its variables. Symmetric functions have deep connections to the representation theories of $GL_n$ and $S_n$, and there are many lovely combinatorial relationships among the five classical, homogeneous bases for symmetric functions. Orellana and Zabrocki introduced inhomogeneous bases of symmetric functions that evaluate to characters of representations of $S_n$. These are called the irreducible character basis and the permutation character basis. These new bases are related to longstanding open problems in combinatorial representation theory including the restriction problem and the Kronecker problem.

In our work, we describe combinatorial interpretations for the transition matrices from the permutation character basis to classical bases for symmetric functions. These interpretations involve Lyndon words and tableaux.

Beth Anne Castellano; Rosa Orellana, Mike Zabrocki
Dartmouth College

Take a standard Young tableau with n cells and place k copies of it corner to corner, each copy above and to the right of the one before. Rectifying with jeu de taquin computes the k-th power of the tableau in the plactic monoid. We give an explicit description of this power whenever k is at least n. Row i begins with a run of copies of the number i. The length of that run is k minus the number of non-descents of the tableau smaller than i. The run is followed by a short tail of larger numbers, one for each non-descent, and the tail is also read off from the non-descents. In particular, the answer depends only on the descent set of the tableau, not on its shape. The argument works for the reading word of any permutation, which is what makes an induction possible. It uses row insertion, Greene’s theorem on decreasing subsequences, and a lemma of Sagan and Zhao.

There is so much data out there, but nearly all of it is functionally inaccessible to the average person. It takes time and resources to find the data, clean it, and organize it into a form that can be analyzed easily. Mid-century polling is no expectation. William Caron; Professor Caitlyn Parmelee
Keene State College

For positive integers r ≤ n, the r-partition graph G[[n], r] has as its vertices all partitions of {1, …, n} into exactly r nonempty parts, with two vertices adjacent when one partition can be obtained from the other by moving a single element from one part to another. The number of vertices is the Stirling number of the second kind S(n, r), and even small cases, such as the 15-vertex, 35-edge graph G[[5], 2], have rich structure. Earlier work established basic invariants such as vertex and edge counts, clique number, and diameter. In this talk we present new results on the chromatic number and planarity of these graphs. We show that the chromatic number of G[[n], 2] is 2 when n is even and 4 when n is odd and at least 5. For planarity, we prove that G[[n], 2] is planar exactly when n ≤ 3, that G[[n], 3] is planar exactly when n = 3 or 4, and that G[[n], n−1] is non-planar for all n ≥ 5. Our non-planarity proofs locate a copy of K₃,₃ in the smallest non-planar case and then extend it to all larger n by adding elements to a fixed part. We conclude with computational evidence for conjectured formulas for vertex connectivity, κ(G[[n], 2]) = n − 1 and κ(G[[n], 3]) = 2n − 4, and discuss open problems. This work was carried out during a summer research program, and the talk requires no background beyond basic graph theory.

Melissa Zhao; Denae Ventura
Mount Holyoke

A plateau in a word is a pair of equal adjacent letters. Two patterns are strongly equivalent if, for every choice of how many times each letter appears, the same number of words avoid each pattern. We prove a conjecture that strongly equivalent permutation patterns are avoided by the same number of words with any given number of plateaus. In fact, we prove this for every pair of strongly equivalent patterns that have no plateaus of their own. The key observation is that collapsing each run of equal letters to a single letter does not change whether a word avoids such a pattern. Building on this, we show that the joint distribution of descents and plateaus is determined by the distribution of descents alone. Using Pollak’s cyclic lemma, we obtain a formula for the distribution of descents and plateaus over pattern-avoiding parking functions. For the twelve patterns strongly equivalent to 1234, this gives the plateau distribution in closed form.

Steven Dahill
Chaminade High School

The Federican Solids is a proposed classification of polyhedra organized around cut and rotate instructions. A polyhedron is divided into two congruent pieces, one piece is rotated, and the pieces are rejoined with their cut faces fully coinciding and without interior overlap. The transformation allows for shapes to undergo a geometric transformation while preserving volume and surface area, with the change in the number of faces. Working models and 3d printed examples make the transformations visible, supporting discussion of symmetry, face counts, and the conditions for membership. Near miss examples help clarify the proposed definition, developed through independent exploration and collaboration with SCSU New Haven math professors. The project also suggests opportunities for hands-on geometry activities and teacher education. Named in Memory of Brown University student, Federico Barrera, It invites us to ask a broader question, How far can familiar mathematics take us when we ask unfamiliar questions?

Noah Izzo; Professor Owen Beissel, Professor Braxton Carrigan
Southern Connecticut State University
5:10-5:25

The standard federal designation of medically underserved areas relies on quantities such as poverty rate, infant mortality, and provider-to-population ratios to draw a conclusion. These measurements take these metrics and condense them into one scalar number that (dis)qualifies an area as underserved. However, the final information encoded retains nothing of how these underserved areas are arranged relative to one another. We use Topological Data Analysis and Persistent Homology to study hospital access based on the hospital locations. To do this, we construct a filtration over drive-time, where a filtration is defined as a growing sequence of shapes that encode the information at a given scale.

Using publicly available data and code, we identified the hospital locations, demographic information, and computed the drive-time between hospitals. From this data, we formed two filtrations: a Vietoris-Rips filtration on the hospital metric space, and a sublevel-set filtration of the access function over a k-nearest-neighbor complex on populated centroids.

We compared hospital access in two states: Arkansas and Massachusetts. Using the topological features of these filtrations, we identified “holes” in access in both states. The holes present in Arkansas persist for over an hour of drivetime, while Massachusetts contains only short lived features. By incorporating population data, we see that the holes in Arkansas’s map contain real populations rather than empty land, whereas Massachusetts's gaps track geographic barriers such as rivers and coastlines. Through comparing facility locations and population data, our approach is able to identify holes in access that affect large populations, thus identifying ideal locations for new healthcare facilities.

Irsa Golemi

Concerns about the leaking pipeline from undergraduate to graduate study in mathematical sciences raise important questions about what shapes students’ decisions to pursue advanced degrees. Understanding the experiences and motivations of prospective graduate students is essential for supporting them and sustaining a robust mathematical science workforce. This qualitative study analyzes personal statements of 29 applicants to mathematics and statistics graduate programs, focusing on the experiences they describe and how they position themselves in relation to them. Using the lens of pivotal experiences and personal agency, we find that applicants rarely frame a single experience as pivotal; instead, they emphasize coursework, research, and teaching as motivational factors. The applicants also tend to portray themselves as active or collaborative participants, actively shaping their decisions to pursue graduate study.

For (x²−1)²+(y²−1)²+ax+by=t with t in (0,1), I compute the largest disk in the (a,b)-plane preserving a nonsingular projective curve with four unnested real ovals. I reduce the singularity equations to one-variable minimization, identify the sharp degenerations, and optimize the radius over t.

Iz Tecúm

One’s attitude toward music is a social-psychological phenomenon shaped by individual experiences and sociocultural contexts. How can it be quantified and categorized? How to describe these data's relationship mathematically? Drawing on the ABC Model of Attitude, my study investigates the affective, behavioral, and cognitive dimensions of attitudes toward Western classical music (instrumental) among Chinese undergraduates studying at U.S. colleges through a mixed-methods framework. The quantitative component uses survey data to examine how dimensions of musical experience are associated with variation in students’ attitudes. Two-sample tests of differences in means, simple linear regression, and forward model selection will be used, with particular emphasis on the relationship between the affective and behavioral components. The qualitative component draws on semi-structured interviews examining students’ musical experiences in greater depth. Thematically coded interview data will be analyzed through code-co-occurrence analyses and represented in heat maps to explore associations between affect and cognition, as well as behavior and cognition. While presenting the statistical methodology and results, I will elaborate on the mathematical mechanisms behind them to provide a theoretical basis of the multidimensional nature of musical attitudes.

Carly Zhang
Mount Holyoke College

In this talk we will discuss a particular symmetry group that can be derived from a structure called a dessin d'enfant (French for “children's drawing”), which is essentially a connected bipartite graph embedded in an oriented surface. The symmetry group is known as the dessin's cartographic group, and is generated by rotations of the edges of the dessin about their incident vertices. We will explore patterns that emerge from taking the cartographic groups of different classes of bipartite graphs, including stars, cycles, paths, caterpillars, and more. Particular attention will be given to prisms, which exhibit a surprising pattern in their cartographic groups.
